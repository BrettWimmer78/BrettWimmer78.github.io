Bed-of-nails compression test fixture

A multidisciplinary team built a fixture for BD that locates and supports a printed circuit board assembly, compresses it, and makes electrical contact with more than 400 test points through spring-loaded pogo pins.

My role: Mechanical Engineering Team Lead

I coordinated the mechanical effort and was the main line of communication between the team and BD sponsors. My primary individual design was the pin plate, which holds the pogo-pin array and locating pins. I laid out its holes and component interfaces from the manufacturer's probe specifications and the fixture's requirements. I also contributed to full-assembly CAD integration, engineering drawings, GD&T, and coordination with external CNC vendors.

Design decisions

  • Applied ASME Y14.5 GD&T and tolerance stack-up analysis to hold positional accuracy across the pin array and prevent pin-to-test-point misalignment.
  • Chose Garolite G-10 for the plate for its dielectric strength and dimensional stability.
  • Applied DFM and DFA constraints so the plate could be made by outside CNC vendors.
  • Ran two SolidWorks Simulation studies of the PCBA under pogo-pin loading to find where added support was needed.

Verification

After fabrication, the mechanical and electrical subsystems were integrated and the fixture was checked for probe contact, alignment, and travel. The completed assembly was functional.

Why it matters

At the start of the project, BD sponsors estimated that preparing each PCBA for testing took about 3 hours of employee time. The fixture replaces that manual preparation with a standardized, repeatable setup for board positioning, compression, and electrical contact.

02 · ME 314 · INDIVIDUAL DESIGN PROJECT · SPRING 2025 · HAND-CALCULATION DESIGN STUDY

Torsion bar suspension

I designed a torsion bar system that carries vertical wheel loads through torsional resistance, sizing each component by hand calculation against stated factor-of-safety requirements. Design inputs: a 15 in lever arm, a 145 in shaft, a 958 lbf maximum axle load, and 106 unidirectional torque cycles at 99.9% reliability.

System schematic.
Spline geometry: 15 teeth, 8/16 pitch, 2.00 in OD.
Bearing support lug geometry.

What I designed

  • Shaft: hollow AISI 4140 Q&T steel, 2.00 in OD by 1.00 in ID.
  • Splines: ANSI B92.1, 15 teeth, checked in shaft shear, tooth shear, and compression.
  • Bearing lug: 330.0 T5 cast aluminum with a Symmco SS-6472-16 sleeve bearing, sized with lug charts for bearing and offset loading.
  • Bolted joints: a stiffness model with preload (75% of proof load, Grade 8 bolt properties) for the lug mount, and an eccentrically loaded shear joint for the fixed coupling.
  • Axle: 4140 steel, with shear and bending moment diagrams.
  • Fatigue: Modified Goodman check at the spline and an S-N curve at the most highly loaded location.

Decisions and trade-offs

  • The spline set the shaft size. Few diameter and pitch combinations met the 1.05 minimum. After a long iteration I settled on 15 teeth at 2.00 in OD, so the shaft is far stronger in static torsion (factor of safety 14.3) than its own requirement of 1.15.
  • Rounded up to a standard size. The axle needed 0.657 in minimum, so I specified a standard 0.75 in bar.
  • Standardized to cut cost. One steel across the major steel parts, a catalog sleeve bearing chosen to limit lug size and trimming, and a hollow shaft to reduce weight and material.
